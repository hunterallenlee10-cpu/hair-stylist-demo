# Top salon sites say less upfront

The best-regarded hair salon, colorist and independent stylist websites treat the home page as a short, image-led doorway and put everything explanatory one tap behind it, which means Mickayla Ramsey's reaction to the Disco Lemonade concept ("kind of a lot on one page") matches how the top of the category already builds. The five hair salon sites that earned an Awwwards Honorable Mention between 2022 and 2026 average about **319 words** on the home page and none exceeds 482; vivid color specialists run from roughly 80 to 650 words in 3 to 9 sections. The current Disco Lemonade home page, at **11 sections, about 850 words, 22 images and 23 planner buttons across 23 phone screens**, has the shape of the long, search-led salon pages rather than the brand-led ones, and it carries on one page the exact items the stronger sites move off it: the service detail, the full gallery, the map, the process and care rules, the promo, the reviews and the FAQ. The recommended change is a hub home page of about six blocks and 250 to 350 words, five named looks instead of an eight-photo gallery, one consultation label used three times instead of 23, and five short sub-pages (Work, Services, New here, The studio, FAQ) that hold what was removed. The site's existing dark base, single pink accent, two typefaces and light motion already fit what the edited sites do, so this is a restructuring job, not a redesign. Two limits apply throughout: none of the sites studied publishes booking or enquiry data, so "best" means design recognition and consistent practice rather than proven conversion, and the teardowns read code and copy, not rendered screens, so visual quality was not observed directly.

## Honorable-mention home pages average 319 words in 6 to 10 blocks

Hair salons rarely win top-tier web awards, so the realistic benchmark is an Awwwards Honorable Mention, which goes to sites the jury scores 6.5 or higher on a rubric weighted 40% design, 30% usability, 20% creativity and 10% content ([Awwwards](https://www.awwwards.com/about-evaluation/)). No hair salon took Site of the Day in 2022 to 2026 on the pages searched; the recognised set is Gielly Green in London ([Awwwards](https://www.awwwards.com/sites/gielly-green)), ALICE Hair & Art in Stockholm ([Awwwards](https://www.awwwards.com/sites/alice-hair-art)), Atelier Brzozowski in Poland ([Awwwards](https://www.awwwards.com/sites/atelier-brzozowski)), Una in Auckland ([Awwwards](https://www.awwwards.com/sites/una-1)) and Il Colorista in Italy ([Awwwards](https://www.awwwards.com/sites/il-colorista-salon)), with Effectownia in Warsaw as a CSS Nectar Site of the Day ([CSS Nectar](https://cssnectar.com/css-gallery-inspiration/effectownia-fryzjer/)). No juried award was found for a solo vivid colorist's site, so the closest models for a one-chair studio come from pairing these with working vivid specialists and celebrity-colorist benchmarks.

Across thirteen sites torn down from their live HTML, ten are small multi-page sites of roughly 4 to 15 pages whose home page acts as a hub of teasers; only ALICE is a true one-pager, and it runs to six blocks. The typical recognised home page holds **6 to 10 content blocks** and a few hundred words. The pattern at the top end is starker: Jenna Perry Hair's home page is a full-bleed image, a "Book now" button and five team names, 23 words in all ([jennaperryhair.com](https://www.jennaperryhair.com/)); Suite Caroline's is a hero line, three products and five captioned color results ([suitecaroline.com](https://www.suitecaroline.com/)); Atelier Brzozowski's award-recognised "business card" home is a single 51-word paragraph ([atelierbrzozowski.com](https://atelierbrzozowski.com/)). Vivid specialists follow the same instinct on cheaper platforms. Heather Dobosz Hair, an NJ vivid specialist, runs a three-section, roughly 80-word home page with three photo tiles ([heatherdoboszhair.com](https://heatherdoboszhair.com)); Nikki the Hair Artist, a private-suite vivid specialist, uses about nine short sections with one hero button ([nikkithehairartist.com](https://www.nikkithehairartist.com)).

Counts for the other sites are from static HTML and include some duplicated mobile markup, so on-screen repetition is usually lower ([una-akl.com](https://www.una-akl.com/), [ilcolorista.salon](https://ilcolorista.salon/), [spokeandweal.com](https://spokeandweal.com/), [effectownia.pl](https://effectownia.pl/), [salonsck.com](https://www.salonsck.com/)).

The sample splits into two legitimate strategies. Brand-led minimal sites win the juried recognition and assume the visitor already knows the name from Instagram or word of mouth. Search-led comprehensive sites such as Effectownia and Salon SCK run to about 1,500 words with per-service paragraphs, embedded reviews and many landing pages, and they earn listicle mentions and local search traffic. The weakest recognised entry, Serenity Hair, mixes the two, with long testimonials on a short site and four different labels for the same booking action ([serenityhairblaxland.com.au](https://serenityhairblaxland.com.au/)). Disco Lemonade currently sits in that mixed middle: longer than every Honorable Mention site, with more sections than any site in the table, yet with a brand whose whole point is personality and a booking route that starts with a text message.

## Prices, policies, FAQ and the full lookbook live one tap away

What the stronger sites leave off the home page is consistent enough to act as a checklist. **Twelve of thirteen** award-level and top-end home pages show no service prices; pricing sits on a page usually called Services or Menu, as on Jenna Perry's menu of one-sentence descriptions with price ranges ([Jenna Perry menu](https://www.jennaperryhair.com/menu)). Cancellation and lateness rules always sit on a dedicated Appointments, Policies or FAQ page, as at Una ([Una appointments](https://www.una-akl.com/pages/appointments)). A FAQ exists as its own page on four of the thirteen sites and appears on no home page ([Spoke & Weal FAQ](https://spokeandweal.com/faq)). The vivid specialists make the same cut: none of them puts session length, cost logic, deposits, fading or aftercare on the home page. Heather Dobosz places all of it in one article under a "Vivid Hair Color" menu item, stating that full-head sessions run 7 to 8 hours at $110 per hour and that an in-person consultation always comes first ([HDH vivids article](https://heatherdoboszhair.com/vivids/blog-post-title-one-chmgf)). Nikki writes hers as client questions ("Why are Vivids so expensive?", "Won't bleach damage my hair?") on a separate vivid FAQ page ([Nikki vivid FAQ](https://www.nikkithehairartist.com/copy-of-vivids)).

The portfolio follows the same rule. Una keeps a separate Lookbook of 133 images with a "Filter by service" control ([Una lookbook](https://www.una-akl.com/pages/lookbook)), while the home page carries only a link tile. The most edited home pages show five or six work images, each named: Suite Caroline's five are titled by client and color result, such as "Platinum For Sabrina" and "Michelle Williams Pink" ([suitecaroline.com](https://www.suitecaroline.com/)), and Shelley Gregory, a Las Vegas colorist, presents 28 looks under names like "Opal", "Cotton Candy" and "Hidden Rainbow" with no other copy ([shelleygregoryhair.com](https://www.shelleygregoryhair.com/portfolio)). Naming is what turns a set of photos into a body of work; a large uncaptioned grid reads as a feed.

Navigation is short and mostly hidden. The recognised sites use 5 to 8 labels, and four of them reduce the visible header to a logo, a "Menu" control and one booking button; Una puts hours, address, parking and Instagram inside the menu overlay instead of giving them a home-page section ([una-akl.com](https://www.una-akl.com/)). A booking control sits in the header on 11 of 13 sites, and the Honorable Mention sites use their booking prompt only **one to three times** on the home page, always with one label. Nielsen Norman Group's testing supports the hidden-menu compromise: show links openly only when there are four or fewer, otherwise hide some and repeat the key destinations as links in the page itself ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/)).

For a consultation-first colorist, the new-client route is the most prominent thing on the site and it has its own page. Nikki's hero button reads "New here? Start >>" and leads to a page that reassures ("Nervous? I get it!"), says who the service is and is not for, frames the process as "Just 2 Steps" and only then offers the form ([Nikki new clients](https://www.nikkithehairartist.com/new-clients)). Heather Dobosz runs the same text-first model Mickayla uses: new clients text a short checklist of name, photos of current hair, inspiration photos and availability, and the color consultation is complimentary ([HDH new client booking](https://heatherdoboszhair.com/new-clientbooking)). Sunday Hair Studio removes a decision from the client entirely: "you don't need to know exactly what service to choose- that's my job." ([Sunday Hair Studio](https://www.sundayhairstudio.com/newguests)). A stylist-business educator puts the principle in one line: "Your website should facilitate a decision, not create more questions." ([The Modern Hairstylist podcast](https://www.buzzsprout.com/1896666/episodes/18033416)). One-person studios handle "about me" and the room the same way, with one identity line on the home page ("Private Spooky Suite", "One Client at a Time") and a personally labelled page behind it ([nikkithehairartist.com](https://www.nikkithehairartist.com), [crishair.com](https://www.crishair.com)).

## Monochrome chrome, two typefaces and five animations keep loud brands calm

The edited sites show restraint in counts more than in any particular style. They use one or two type families and two to four weights. Spoke & Weal declares exactly two families, Instrument Serif and Inter, tops its largest heading out near 63px, sets secondary labels in tiny letter-spaced capitals and caps text columns by character count so short copy looks deliberate ([spokeandweal.com](https://spokeandweal.com/)). Most blocks hold a heading of 2 to 13 words and at most one short paragraph. Very large type appears only on two or three words at a time. The counter-example is Fox & Jane, which declares about 25 font families and 84 stock animations ([foxandjanesalon.com](https://foxandjanesalon.com/)).

Pacing comes from giving each block one job (a statement, a media piece, a list or a proof) and separating blocks with alternating full-width dark and light bands instead of cards and borders. Spoke & Weal's ten sections alternate "ink" and "cream" backgrounds, and its hero is exactly one screen tall with a five-word headline and two links ([spokeandweal.com](https://spokeandweal.com/)). Numbered lists and short eyebrow labels give the same rhythm on template builds such as Effectownia's ([effectownia.pl](https://effectownia.pl/)).

The colorful brands that read as well designed keep the interface almost colorless and let color arrive through one channel. Starface's theme is black, white and a single yellow, with zero keyframe animations and the personality carried in lowercase copy ([starface.world](https://starface.world/)). Good Dye Young and Pulp Riot, both vivid hair color brands, run overwhelmingly black-and-white stylesheets and leave the color to the product and hair photography ([gooddyeyoung.com](https://gooddyeyoung.com/), [pulpriothair.com](https://www.pulpriothair.com/)). Clutter tracks element count, not palette: Chillhouse has a disciplined four-color palette but 155 image tags, 16 videos and 2,647 words on its home page ([chillhouse.com](https://chillhouse.com/)). A black-based site with one accent and one confident typeface is also the underused route in a sector crowded with beige editorial templates, which matters under the Awwwards-side test that a concept should be one that "couldn't be swapped onto any other brand" ([Hon Tran](https://www.hontran.dev/blog/awwwards-judging-criteria)). That source is one practitioner's account of the rubric, not an official Awwwards statement.

Motion on the calm sites is a handful of fades and slow transitions. Spoke & Weal defines five animations in total, mostly 500ms, honors reduced-motion settings and gives its background video a pause control ([spokeandweal.com](https://spokeandweal.com/)). Nielsen Norman Group found most study participants were at least mildly disoriented by scrolljacking and advises against it on mobile ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)). The same practitioner source says stacked effects read to jurors "as a lack of control" and that "a single strong idea carried through every section beats three competing ideas" ([Hon Tran](https://www.hontran.dev/blog/awwwards-judging-criteria)).

## The average phone visitor stops near screen ten of 23

Usability evidence explains the owner's reaction without relying on taste. Attention drops steeply with depth: in Nielsen Norman Group's eyetracking, 57% of viewing time fell above the fold and 74% within the first two screenfuls ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). That study is desktop only and dates from 2018. The best current mobile figure is Contentsquare's 2026 benchmark, in which the average mobile visitor scrolls **45.2%** of a page and spends 2 minutes 20 seconds per session ([Contentsquare](https://contentsquare.com/guides/digital-experience-benchmark/engagement/)). Applied to a 23-screen page, that puts the average visitor at about screen ten, so the later sections of the current order (the how-it-works steps, the reviews, the FAQ and the final call to action) sit in territory a large share of visitors never reach. The benchmark comes from large enterprise sites, so treat this as rough arithmetic and not a measurement of this page.

Three further mechanisms apply. People judge visual complexity within 50 milliseconds and prefer simple, familiar layouts ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/)). Extraneous cognitive load comes from redundant links and from making people read, remember and decide more than they need to ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/minimize-cognitive-load/)); 23 buttons that all open the same planner are redundant links in exactly that sense. And more options cause people to defer a decision when the task is hard and they are unsure what they want ([Kellogg summary of Chernev et al. 2015](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual)), which describes a new color client facing a five-stop dial, a seven-tile grid and a multi-step planner on one page. The choice-overload effect is contested: an earlier meta-analysis found a mean effect near zero ([Scheibehenne et al. 2010](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/can-there-ever-be-too-many-options-a-meta-analytic-review-of-choice-overload-scheibehenne-benjamin/10008756224)). No controlled study gives a correct number of sections or buttons, and no independent evidence shows that quizzes, sliders or configurators raise enquiries on a small local service site; the figures in circulation come from companies that sell quiz and form software.

The long page has a real case, and the fix keeps what is right about it. Shoppers on phones scroll a home page to judge the full scope of an offer and assume a narrow display means a narrow business ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-homepage-provide-full-scope)), so a short home page must still name everything Mickayla does, including blonding and lived-in color. Salon clients say what they want from a site is a contact page with location and hours (56%), reviews (46%), services with prices (40%) and before-and-after photos (36%), with blogs and trend news at 4% ([Phorest](https://www.phorest.com/?p=60234)); that survey is vendor-run, from 2019, with no published sample size. More than half of consumers (54%) visit a business's website after reading a positive review ([BrightLocal](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/)), so most visitors arrive half-persuaded and want confirmation plus a next step. Local SEO practitioners rank a dedicated page for each service as the top local organic factor while saying "massive pages of content are no longer the way" ([Whitespark](https://whitespark.ca/local-search-ranking-factors/)). Usability and search evidence therefore point the same way: several short, focused pages beat one long one.

## A six-block home page and five short sub-pages fit Disco Lemonade

The recommendation is to keep every idea on the current page and change where each one lives. The home page keeps one job: confirm who she is, what she specializes in and where, show a small set of named work, and send a stranger to the consultation. A working budget, derived from the award sample and the copy-length evidence and not from a measured benchmark, is **six blocks, 250 to 350 words, seven or eight images and one consultation label used three times**. Landing-page data for professional services places the useful band at roughly 275 to 745 words and shows plain wording mattering more than length ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/professional-services-conversion-rate/)). At that budget the page should run to well under half its current 23 screens; no pixel heights were measured for the reference sites, so screen count is a target to check in the browser, not a sourced figure.

The six blocks, in order, are a one-screen hero with a single real photo, an identity line that states vivid color specialist, private one-chair studio and Leonardtown, and one button; a one-sentence statement in Mickayla's voice with a "Meet Mickayla" link; five named looks in a uniform crop; the seven services as names only, with one link to Services; a three-step "how to start" strip (text CONSULTATION, free consult, color day) with the honest line that big transformations take most of a day; and a closing block with the button, the address, "by appointment" and directions. The five named looks solve three current sections at once. The Flavor Dial's stops (Vanilla, A pop, Panels, Color block, Full rainbow) are already names in Mickayla's voice, so presenting them as five numbered, captioned photos gives the home page its curated portfolio, its service overview and its signature idea in one static block.

Three of these calls need a word of justification. First, the reviews: placeholder content is a named mistake in award-side commentary ([Hon Tran](https://www.hontran.dev/blog/awwwards-judging-criteria)), and the top-end sites that omit client reviews substitute press, which a new studio does not have, so one real review is worth more than three sample ones. The project's own notes record that no Google Business Profile was found for the studio; since the profile's category, proximity and reviews dominate map results ([Whitespark](https://whitespark.ca/local-search-ranking-factors/)), creating one and collecting reviews there does more for discovery than any on-site review block. Second, the model call addresses a different audience from paying new clients, and mixed audiences on client-facing pages are a recurring clutter signal in the colorist sample ([Bold Hearts new guest page](https://www.boldheartsstudio.com/new-guest)). Third, the Flavor Dial is the most distinctive thing on the site and deserves to survive, but as an interactive step it duplicates the first question of the consultation planner, so it works best as the opening of the New here page, where choosing a flavor leads straight into the request.

The consultation route should be simplified along with the page. The site's code shows a mobile bar with up to four controls (back to top, Text, Call, Plan My Hair) and a planner that runs five steps and requires a phone number at the last one. Expert guidance on persistent bars is to keep them only as tall as readable, tappable controls require and to question whether each fixed element earns the screen space it takes ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)), so the bar should carry two controls at most: the primary consultation button and a plain "Text" link. Form analytics identify phone and email fields as the highest-abandonment fields after passwords ([Zuko](https://www.zuko.io/blog/which-form-fields-cause-the-biggest-ux-problems)) and staged forms work best when steps are few and independent ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)); because the planner's output is a pre-filled text sent from the client's own phone, the phone field is worth removing and the flow worth trimming to two or three light steps (the look, the hair now, anything else). The planner should sit on the New here page as the guided option beside the plain instruction to text CONSULTATION, which mirrors the text-with-checklist model already used by a comparable vivid specialist. The navigation becomes five real pages (Work, Services, New here, The studio, FAQ) behind one menu control, with the consultation button always visible beside it and the address, hours, Instagram and gift card link inside the menu overlay and footer.

The visual system needs tightening, not replacing. The code shows a near-black base, one hot pink accent, a lemon yellow, the Fraunces display serif with DM Sans, and motion confined to the hero and the dial with two small CSS animations. That is already the "black base, one accent, two families, about five animations" recipe the edited sites use. The remaining discipline is to make pink the only interactive color, reserve lemon for the logo mark and one moment per page, let client hair supply every other color, hold headings to a dozen words and each block to one short paragraph, and review decorative frames and dividers against the one-job-per-block rule. Pricing is the open content question: the site currently says cost is quoted at the consultation, clients rank accurate prices among their top wants, and vivid specialists answer with an hourly rate, a "starting at" figure or a fixed first-visit package rather than a full menu. Which of those Mickayla is comfortable publishing is her decision, and the Services page should carry it once she makes it.

## Conclusion

The useful finding is that the owner's instinct and the jury's taste are the same instinct. A top-end stylist site earns its calm by trusting that a visitor who arrives from Instagram or a review needs confirmation and a door, and by trusting sub-pages to do the explaining for the minority who want it. For Disco Lemonade that reframes the Flavor Dial, the planner and the gallery from competing features on one page into a sequence across pages: named looks on the home page create the want, the New here page turns the want into a text, and the Work, Services and FAQ pages answer the doubts. The same split serves local search, because short service-focused pages naming Leonardtown are what practitioners expect to rank.

What remains unknown is whether a first-time client reacts the way the owner did, since no study separates the two and none of the reference sites publishes results. The cheapest way to settle it is to put the shortened home page in front of three to five real prospective clients on their own phones and watch where they tap and where they stop, then track consultation texts received before and after. A visual review of the reference sites on a phone is also still owed, because this research read their structure and code and could not see their photography, spacing or motion in use.
